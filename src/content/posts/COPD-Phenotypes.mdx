One person can have a lot of emphysema and almost no sputum.

Someone else can have thick airways, a chronic cough, and frequent flares, with much less emphysema.

A third person can already have a lot of small-airway trouble before spirometry looks dramatically bad.

All three may still get called **COPD**.

That is the first thing worth keeping in mind:

> **COPD is a diagnosis. It is not one phenotype, and it is not one number.**

If you work on imaging or virtual imaging trials, this matters because an endpoint has to match the actual question. If you use FEV$_1$ as a stand-in for emphysema, or LAA-950 as a stand-in for symptoms, you are asking one measurement to do a job it was not built for.

A cleaner picture is

$$
\text{cause}
\rightarrow
\text{pathology}
\rightarrow
\text{phenotype}
\rightarrow
\text{physiology}
\rightarrow
\text{symptoms and outcomes},
$$

and different tests look at different parts of that chain.

## 1. Diagnosis is not phenotype

COPD is a mixed lung condition: lasting respiratory symptoms plus airflow obstruction, tied to airways, alveoli, or both. GOLD still uses spirometry to show that the obstruction is persistent, and post-bronchodilator numbers are used to confirm the diagnosis.[1]

A handy shorthand is

$$
\mathrm{COPD}
=
\mathrm{airflow\ obstruction}
+
\mathrm{clinical\ context}.
$$

Once you have the diagnosis, at least four different questions are still open:

1. **What caused it?**
2. **What structural disease is sitting there?**
3. **How impaired is this person?**
4. **How much does it mess with daily life and future risk?**

Those questions are related. They are not the same question.

### Etiotype

An **etiotype** is why the disease showed up: cigarettes, pollution or biomass smoke, lungs that never fully developed, genetics such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, an old infection, asthma-linked disease, or something else.

### Phenotype

A **phenotype** is the pattern you can actually observe. In COPD that includes emphysema-heavy disease, airway-heavy disease, chronic bronchitis, small-airway disease, mucus plugging, frequent exacerbations, and pulmonary vascular involvement.

### Biomarker or surrogate

A **biomarker** is just a measurable feature of a biological state.

A **surrogate endpoint** is a much bigger claim. It is a biomarker you are willing to treat as a stand-in for a clinical outcome.

That gap is easy to forget and expensive if you do.

> **LAA-950, Pi10, FEV$_1$, and 6-minute walk distance are measurements. Do not automatically call them validated surrogates for death, symptoms, or treatment benefit.**

## 2. Think in phenotypes, not one COPD axis

The useful phenotypes overlap. That is the point.

### Emphysema-predominant COPD

**Biology:** alveolar walls break down and distal airspaces get larger.

**Symptoms that often show up first:** getting winded on exertion, poorer exercise tolerance, sometimes surprisingly little sputum.

**Qualitative CT:**

- centrilobular emphysema
- panlobular emphysema
- paraseptal emphysema
- upper- versus lower-lung predominance
- bullae
- hyperinflation

The Fleischner visual classes give a shared language for those patterns and grades, and they track with physiology and later outcomes.[6]

**Quantitative CT:**

$$
\mathrm{LAA}_{-950}
=
100
\frac{N(\mathrm{HU} \lt -950)}
{N_{\mathrm{lung}}},
$$

where a larger inspiratory LAA-950 usually means more low-attenuation, emphysema-looking lung.

Another one is the density percentile:

$$
P_{15}
=
15^{\mathrm{th}}\text{ percentile of the lung-density histogram}.
$$

A **more negative** Perc15 usually means lower-density lung and more destruction.

These numbers care a lot about how deep the breath was, how you reconstructed, slice thickness, scanner calibration, and noise. A HU threshold is not independent of the imaging chain.

### Airway-predominant COPD

**Biology:** wall inflammation, remodeling, narrowing, and a lumen that no longer looks like the original tube.

**Symptoms that often show up first:** cough, sputum, wheeze, dyspnea, that recurring bronchitic picture.

**Qualitative CT:**

- bronchial wall thickening
- lumen narrowing
- bronchiectasis when it is there
- visible mucus
- airway distortion

**Quantitative CT:**

For one airway cross-section,

$$
WA\%
=
100
\frac{A_{\mathrm{wall}}}
{A_{\mathrm{wall}}+A_{\mathrm{lumen}}}.
$$

Other branch-level numbers include

- wall thickness, WT
- wall area, WA
- lumen area, LA
- inner and outer diameter
- hydraulic diameter
- tapering
- branch count
- how far out the tree you can still see

A common summary is **Pi10**, the estimated square-root wall area for a made-up airway with a 10 mm inner perimeter. Higher Pi10 usually means thicker walls. COPDGene linked higher Pi10 with worse FEV$_1$, a shorter 6-minute walk, and worse respiratory health status.[7]

If you work on imaging, treat airway numbers with extra suspicion:

> **Small-airway measurements can move when the CT changes, even if the airway did not.**

Voxel size, slice thickness, kernel, denoising, dose, partial volume, and the segmentation code can all push WT and WA% around.

### Chronic-bronchitis phenotype

The classic clinical definition is cough and sputum for at least 3 months a year, in 2 years in a row.[8]

This one is mainly **clinical**. It is not a CT threshold.

Useful things to count include

- how often they cough
- how much sputum
- what the sputum looks like
- CAAT/CAT items
- how often they flare
- mucus burden on CT

Chronic bronchitis and CT-visible wall disease overlap. They are not the same thing.

### Small-airway-disease phenotype

The small conducting airways are a big source of resistance in COPD. A lot of them sit below what ordinary CT can resolve.

So we often measure them **sideways**.

Useful physiology includes

- expiratory flows
- lung volumes
- RV/TLC
- oscillometry
- ventilation heterogeneity

Useful imaging includes expiratory air trapping and parametric response mapping.

A simple expiratory threshold looks like

$$
\mathrm{LAA}_{-856,\ exp}
=
100
\frac{N(\mathrm{HU} \lt -856 \text{ on expiration})}
{N_{\mathrm{lung}}}.
$$

People use this a lot in research as “low attenuation on expiration.” It is also very sensitive to how hard the person exhaled and how you scanned.

If you register inspiration and expiration, **parametric response mapping (PRM)** labels matching voxels. The classic PRM split is emphysema versus non-emphysematous gas trapping, which people read as functional small-airway disease.[9]

In words,

$$
\mathrm{PRM}_{fSAD}
=
\text{non-emphysematous lung that stays too dark on expiration}.
$$

That is why PRM is appealing when the question is *where small-airway dysfunction lives before the tissue is obviously destroyed*.

### Mucus-plugging phenotype

Mucus plugs are not just a visual annoyance.

CT-visible plugs that block airways have been tied to worse lung function, worse respiratory health status, and mortality in COPD cohorts.[10]

Things you can count include

- plug present or not
- how many segments are involved
- a plug score
- plug volume
- percent of branches occluded
- which generation and which lobe
- how the burden changes over time

One imaging catch: **seeing it is not the same as the biology**. Thicker slices, smoother kernels, more noise, and a limited airway segmentation can make the same plug look smaller, or make it vanish.

### Frequent-exacerbator phenotype

An exacerbation is a **clinical event**. It is not an imaging pattern.

Useful counts include

- moderate exacerbations per year
- severe exacerbations or hospitalizations per year
- time to the next one
- exacerbation rate
- days alive and out of hospital
- rescue-medication use

GOLD 2026 changed A/B/E so that **one moderate exacerbation in the prior year is enough for Group E**, because even one of those raises later risk.[1]

### Pulmonary-vascular phenotype

COPD can also remodel the pulmonary vessels and lead to pulmonary hypertension.

Measurements may include

- pulmonary artery diameter
- pulmonary artery-to-aorta ratio, PA:A
- small-vessel volume
- perfusion metrics
- echo estimates
- right-heart catheterization when that is actually indicated

A PA:A ratio above 1 on CT has been linked to severe exacerbations. Treat it as a risk-related imaging biomarker, not as a diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension by itself.[11]

## 3. Symptoms are another measurement domain

COPD can look ugly on CT and feel mild. It can also feel terrible with a milder-looking scan.

Typical symptoms include

- dyspnea
- chronic cough
- sputum
- wheezing
- chest tightness
- poor exercise tolerance
- fatigue
- sleep trouble
- activity limits
- flare-ups

FEV$_1$ does not swallow all of that.

### mMRC dyspnea scale

The modified Medical Research Council scale is mostly about how much **breathlessness** gets in the way of activity.

It is simple and useful. It is also deliberately narrow.

A higher score means more activity limitation from dyspnea.

### CAAT / CAT

GOLD 2026 says **Chronic Airways Assessment Test (CAAT)**. A lot of older COPD papers still say CAT. Same 8-item, 0-40 questionnaire. Higher is worse health status.[1]

A score around 10 is the usual GOLD split between lower and greater symptom burden.

### SGRQ

The St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire is longer and still widely used in research.

Higher SGRQ means worse respiratory health status.

For a VIT, these patient-reported scores are usually **clinical anchors on the outside**. A CT simulator does not spit them out.

## 4. Spirometry measures obstruction, not the whole disease

The main spirometry numbers are

$$
FEV_1
=
\text{volume forcibly exhaled in the first second},
$$

$$
FVC
=
\text{forced vital capacity},
$$

and

$$
\frac{FEV_1}{FVC}.
$$

GOLD uses post-bronchodilator spirometry to confirm that the obstruction is still there.[1]

After the diagnosis is in, people still grade airflow obstruction with post-bronchodilator FEV$_1$ percent predicted:

| GOLD airflow grade | FEV$_1$ % predicted | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| GOLD 1 | $\ge 80\%$ | mild airflow obstruction |
| GOLD 2 | $50-79\%$ | moderate |
| GOLD 3 | $30-49\%$ | severe |
| GOLD 4 | $\lt 30\%$ | very severe |

This table is useful.

It is also incomplete.

Two people with the same FEV$_1$ can still differ in

- emphysema burden
- airway disease
- symptoms
- exercise capacity
- flare risk
- oxygenation
- other illnesses
- outlook

That is why COPD severity is more like a **set of coordinates** than a single ruler.

## 5. Lung volumes show air trapping and hyperinflation

Spirometry tells you how fast air leaves.

Lung-volume testing asks how much air is still sitting there.

The big ones are

- TLC: total lung capacity
- RV: residual volume
- FRC: functional residual capacity
- inspiratory capacity, IC
- RV/TLC

In obstructive disease, **larger RV and RV/TLC** usually mean more trapped gas.

Hyperinflation can also shrink inspiratory capacity and make people very short of breath on exertion, even when FEV$_1$ has not moved much.

A useful reminder:

$$
\text{obstruction}
\neq
\text{air trapping}
\neq
\text{hyperinflation}.
$$

They are connected. They are not identical.

## 6. DLCO is asking a different question

DLCO, the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, estimates gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary system.

A lower DLCO can mean

- less gas-exchange surface from emphysema
- pulmonary vascular disease
- anemia or other whole-body effects
- some mix of those

In emphysema-heavy COPD, DLCO often tells you something spirometry cannot.

Again:

> FEV$_1$ describes airflow. DLCO describes gas transfer.

A careful imaging study should not treat one as if it were the other.

## 7. Functional performance connects the lung to the person

The **6-minute walk test (6MWT)** is about what someone can actually do.

The main outcome is

$$
6MWD = \text{distance walked in 6 minutes}.
$$

Lower 6MWD tracks with worse outcomes in chronic respiratory disease. Continuous pulse oximetry can catch the lowest SpO$_2$, and you can score dyspnea and fatigue before and after. ATS/ERS standards also remind you that the test is picky about method and has a learning effect, so repeats have to be done the same way.[4]

Other functional pieces include

- desaturation on exercise
- heart-rate response
- Borg dyspnea
- cardiopulmonary exercise testing
- peak oxygen uptake
- daily activity from a wearable

These answer a practical question:

> **What can this person actually do?**

## 8. Composite scores mash domains on purpose

The **BODE index** puts together

- **B**ody mass index
- airflow **O**bstruction
- **D**yspnea
- **E**xercise capacity

It exists because FEV$_1$ alone does not capture prognosis as well as a few dimensions together.[12]

That is a useful lesson for VIT work.

One biomarker is tempting because it is easy to simulate and easy to plot.

A patient outcome is multidimensional because the disease is.

## 9. A metric atlas for COPD studies

The table below is a working map. It is not a claim that every study needs every metric.

| Domain | Metric | Modality/test | What it mainly measures | Typical direction with greater disease burden* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Airflow | FEV$_1$ | spirometry | expiratory airflow | ↓ |
| Airflow | FEV$_1$/FVC | spirometry | obstruction | ↓ |
| Volume | RV | plethysmography | trapped gas | ↑ |
| Volume | RV/TLC | plethysmography | relative air trapping | ↑ |
| Hyperinflation | IC | spirometry/lung volume | inspiratory reserve | often ↓ |
| Gas exchange | DLCO | diffusion test | alveolar-capillary transfer | often ↓ |
| Oxygenation | resting SpO$_2$ | pulse oximetry | oxygen saturation | may ↓ |
| Exercise | 6MWD | 6MWT | functional exercise capacity | ↓ |
| Exercise | nadir SpO$_2$ | 6MWT | exertional desaturation | ↓ |
| Symptoms | mMRC | questionnaire | dyspnea limitation | ↑ |
| Health status | CAAT/CAT | questionnaire | respiratory health burden | ↑ |
| Health status | SGRQ | questionnaire | respiratory quality-of-life impairment | ↑ |
| Events | exacerbations/year | clinical record | disease activity/risk | ↑ |
| Events | hospitalizations/year | clinical record | severe exacerbation burden | ↑ |
| Emphysema | LAA-950 | inspiratory CT | low-density emphysematous lung | ↑ |
| Emphysema | Perc15 | inspiratory CT | lung-density distribution | more negative / ↓ |
| Air trapping | LAA-856 exp | expiratory CT | low attenuation on expiration | ↑ |
| Air trapping | E/I density ratio | paired CT | incomplete expiratory density change | ↑ |
| Small airways | PRM-fSAD | registered insp/exp CT | non-emphysematous gas trapping | ↑ |
| Airways | WT | CT | wall thickness | often ↑ |
| Airways | WA% | CT | wall fraction | often ↑ |
| Airways | lumen area | CT | airway caliber | may ↓ |

## 10. CT gives you structure - but the imaging chain moves the number

For quantitative COPD CT, what you observe is not just biology:

$$
M_{\mathrm{CT}}
=
f(
\text{disease},
\text{inspiration},
\text{dose},
\text{resolution},
\text{noise},
\text{kernel},
\text{slice thickness},
\text{segmentation}
).
$$

That hits different biomarkers in different ways.

### Density metrics

Emphysema and air-trapping numbers care about

- lung inflation
- scanner calibration
- dose and noise
- reconstruction kernel
- slice thickness
- iterative or deep-learning reconstruction

### Airway metrics

WT and WA% are extra sensitive to

- voxel size
- spatial resolution
- airway orientation
- partial volume
- airway segmentation
- how sharp the reconstruction is

### PRM

PRM also depends on

- reproducible inspiration and expiration
- deformable registration
- density calibration
- the thresholds you picked

### Mucus

Mucus numbers also depend on

- airway-lumen segmentation
- HU rules
- object-size cutoff
- how you define connectivity
- resolution
- partial volume

This is exactly where a VIT earns its keep.

If the COPD phantom stays the same and only the CT acquisition changes, then

$$
\Delta M
=
M_{\mathrm{protocol\ B}}
-
M_{\mathrm{protocol\ A}}
$$

can tell you **measurement bias from imaging**, not biological progression.

## 11. Let the phenotype pick the endpoint

Suppose the hypothesis is:

> “A higher-resolution CT reconstruction improves quantitative assessment of COPD.”

That is too broad.

Which piece of COPD?

For **emphysema**, pick something like

$$
|\mathrm{LAA}_{-950}^{CT} - \mathrm{LAA}_{-950}^{GT}|
$$

or Perc15 bias.

For **airway remodeling**, pick

$$
\Delta WT
=
WT_{CT}-WT_{GT}
$$

and

$$
\Delta WA\%
=
WA\%_{CT}-WA\%_{GT}.
$$

For **mucus**, pick object-level sensitivity, false positives, plug-volume error, or a segment-level plug score.

For **small-airway disease**, pick expiratory air-trapping error or PRM classification error.

A good study question looks like

$$
\boxed{
\text{phenotype}
+
\text{measurement}
+
\text{imaging perturbation}
+
\text{ground truth}
}
$$

not just “COPD image quality.”

## 12. A graduate-level exercise

Pick **one COPD phenotype** and design a quantitative experiment.

Before you choose a scanner protocol, write down:

1. phenotype
2. biological ground truth
3. qualitative look
4. quantitative biomarker
5. clinical or physiologic correlate
6. expected direction as disease gets worse
7. important confounders
8. imaging sources of measurement bias
9. primary endpoint
10. validation experiment

For example:

```yaml
phenotype:
  name: "airway-predominant COPD"

ground_truth:
  wall_thickness_mm: known
  lumen_area_mm2: known
  mucus_objects: known

imaging_factors:
  pixel_size_mm: [0.35, 0.50, 0.75]
  slice_thickness_mm: [0.25, 0.625, 1.0]
  reconstruction: ["medium", "sharp"]
  dose: ["low", "reference"]

quantitative_endpoints:
  primary: "absolute WT error"
  secondary:
    - "WA% signed error"
    - "branch detection rate"
    - "mucus object sensitivity"

clinical_anchor:
  examples:
    - "FEV1"
    - "CAAT"
    - "exacerbation history"

analysis:
  design: "paired by identical virtual airway"
  question: "Which imaging factor changes measurement accuracy?"
```

The scanner does not define the experiment.

The disease model does not define the experiment.

The metric does not define the experiment.

**The link among phenotype, ground truth, measurement, and clinical meaning does.**
